7 Independence#

Independence is a fundamental concept in probability theory. It is a measure of the relationship between two events. Two events are independent if the occurrence of one event does not affect the occurrence of the other event.

Mutually Exclusivity

Two events \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive if they cannot occure at the same time. In other words, if \(A\) occurs, then \(B\) cannot occur and vice versa.

If we calculate the probability \(P(A \cup B)\) of two mutually exclusive events, we can write:

\[ P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) \]

In general, the probability of the union of two events is the sum of the probabilities of the events minus the probability of the intersection of the two events:

\[ P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) \]

But, if the events are mutually exclusive, then the probability of the intersection of the two events is 0.

Independence

Two events \(A\) and \(B\) are independent if the occurrence of one event does not affect the occurrence of the other event. In other words:

\[ P(A|B) = P(A) \]

Probability of the Intersection of two Independent Events

If two events are independent, then the probability of the intersection of the two events is the product of the probabilities of the events:

\[ P(AB) = P(A)P(B) \]

Probability of the Intersection of two Independent Events

\[ P(AB) = P(A|B)P(B) \underbrace{=}_{P(A|B) = P(A)} P(A)P(B) \]

If A is independent of B, then B is independent of A

\[ P(A|B) = P(A) \implies P(B|A) = \frac{P(AB)}{P(A)} = \frac{P(A)P(B)}{P(A)} = P(B) \]

If \(A\) is independent of \(B\), then \(A\) is independent of \(B^c\)

\[\begin{split} \begin{align*} P(AB^c) &= P(A) - P(AB) & \text{(by the law of total probability)} \\ &= P(A) - P(A)P(B) & \text{(by independence)} \\ &= P(A)(1 - P(B)) & \\ &= P(A)P(B^c) & \text{(identity axiom)} \end{align*} \end{split}\]

Why This Matters for Statistics#

In statistics, we often assume independence between variables — sometimes without checking it. But this assumption has huge consequences: it simplifies models, enables factorizations, and affects inference.

For example, in linear regression, we assume that the errors are independent. In Naive Bayes classifiers, we assume that features are conditionally independent given the class. These assumptions make the math tractable — but if they’re wrong, our models can become misleading.

Let’s look at a toy example.

Example: Dependent vs. Independent Events#

Suppose we’re modeling two events:

  • \(A\): It’s raining

  • \(B\): The sidewalk is wet

If we assume independence, then we model:

\[ P(\text{Wet} \mid \text{Rain}) = P(\text{Wet}) \quad \text{(wrong!)} \]

But in reality:

\[ P(\text{Wet} \mid \text{Rain}) > P(\text{Wet}) \]

So assuming independence would underestimate the predictive value of rain.

In modeling terms, independence is not just a definition — it’s a modeling assumption that must be justified. Failing to do so can lead to incorrect likelihoods, underestimated uncertainty, or bad predictions.

That’s why we emphasize it here: understanding what independence really means helps you recognize when your models are silently making strong assumptions about the world.
